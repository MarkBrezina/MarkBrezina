Management information and reporting automation (self-initiated) • Manual call log and call-reason taxonomy (precursor to the tools below). Identified and corrected for systematic censoring in the raw series — own break and shift boundaries suppressing apparent demand — before using the findings. • KPI reporting pipeline. Designed a repeat-call reason-logging tool for the Danish market: single-click capture in the agent application with access control and undo, feeding a dashboard of reasons, trends and per-agent patterns used to identify root causes of repeat contact. • Earlier tools. Tools: Google Apps Script (JavaScript) · Google Sheets / Slides / Forms automation · Python · AS400 / IBM i (5250) · data reconciliation and QA · KPI design · Bayesian modelling · client and stakeholder reporting Portfolio write-up Building the management information layer for a multi-market contact centre I was hired as a phone advisor and ended up building the reporting layer the account runs on: a daily pipeline with its own reconciliation controls, a set of capture tools that instrument things the telephony platform does not measure, and a Bayesian model for scheduling outbound calls. The situation. The operation handles inbound sales and service for a holiday-resort operator across the Benelux and Nordic markets, working from a legacy IBM i booking system. Agent performance data arrived as six spreadsheet exports from a central BI team, covering daily, week-to-date and month-to-date figures across the sales and service queues. Turning those into the numbers the operation actually managed by was manual, and for a period it ran through an AI summarisation step that produced figures nobody could reconcile against the source. That is what I set out to replace, and it is why the replacement is deterministic by design: the same inputs produce the same outputs, and every published figure traces back to the row it came from. Starting where there was no data at all. Before any of the reporting work, I spent months hand-logging my own calls — reason, timestamp, free-text note. The telephony platform recorded volumes and handle times but nothing about why people were calling, which is the only thing that lets you reduce repeat contact rather than merely measure it. Coding the free text afterwards produced a taxonomy of around a dozen call reasons. That taxonomy is now the click-capture scheme in a first-call-resolution tool the Danish team uses: one button per reason, logged against agent and hour, feeding a dashboard of trends and root causes. The same log gave the first picture of demand by hour. The most useful thing in that log was an error in it. The first hourly demand profile had a clean dip in the middle of the day. It was my lunch break. The series counted calls I had handled and I was reading it as calls customers had made, and those diverge wherever my own availability varies — breaks, shift boundaries, and more subtly the time spent on long calls, during which genuinely busy periods register as quiet ones. The correction was to change the denominator from clock hours to minutes actually available and to drop buckets with partial coverage. A small fix, but the kind of error that survives into a decision if nobody goes looking for it, and it set the standard I held the rest of the work to. The reporting pipeline. It runs each morning and rebuilds a seven-tab KPI pack: agent-level conversion, revenue per call, average booking value, service revenue share, satisfaction and handling time, across two market groups and both queues, benchmarked against a peer site and scored against per-segment targets. Most of the engineering is not in the metrics. It is in everything around them: • Every figure is reconciled against the source system's own subtotals before publication, and a mismatch is reported rather than printed. • Cross-period consistency is checked per agent — a day cannot exceed its week, a week cannot exceed the month. • Missing, stale or unreadable source files surface as banners on the report instead of appearing as zeros. • Period boundaries are handled explicitly: ISO-week logic with an automatic Monday reset, and month rollover for the cumulative tabs. • An incremental archive lets the week-to-date daily view rebuild from single-day extracts, with source data always taking precedence over archived rows, so re-running the sync is idempotent. • The upstream republishes all six files under new identifiers every day, so files are resolved by folder and name rather than by identifier, with format conversion and timezone-safe date parsing — and an audit log recording exactly which file version produced each figure. Why the controls matter more than the metrics. A reporting tool that is right ninety-five percent of the time is worse than no tool, because the wrong five percent is indistinguishable from the rest and people act on it anyway. The rule I settled on is that the pipeline should fail visibly rather than publish a plausible number. In practice that means a missing source file produces a flagged gap, not a quiet zero, and a figure that will not reconcile against its own source stops the figure, not the check. The call-timing model. Outbound welcome calls need a schedule, and the naive answer — call when past calls were answered most — overfits badly when each weekday-hour cell holds only a handful of observations. I modelled outcomes per cell as Dirichlet–multinomial, which shrinks sparse cells toward the overall rate, with exponential decay so older months count for less as calling patterns drift. Slots are then ranked by expected value, weighing contact likelihood against the cost of reaching someone at a bad moment, rather than by raw contact rate. What this work is. It is analytics engineering: building and owning the pipeline, the data model and the controls that stand between raw operational exports and a figure somebody makes a decision on. It sits close to the model validation work I have done elsewhere, and for the same reason — the discipline is not building the thing, it is being able to say precisely why you trust its output, and being willing to publish nothing when you cannot.
